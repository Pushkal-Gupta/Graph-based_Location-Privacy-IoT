\begin{abstract}
Smart cities depend on continuous location reporting from IoT devices, yet the
privacy mechanisms that protect these reports are evaluated in isolation, with
incompatible datasets, metrics, and threat models, so no fair comparison exists.
We make two contributions that go beyond benchmarking. First, we replace the
ad-hoc privacy scores common in the literature with an \emph{adversary-grounded}
privacy metric: privacy is the expected error of an optimal Bayesian adversary,
measured in metres and therefore directly comparable across mechanism families.
Crucially, we evaluate every mechanism under \emph{two} threat models---a
single-observation snapshot adversary and a Viterbi trajectory-tracking
adversary equipped with a Markov mobility prior---and show that a single privacy
scale is inadequate: mechanisms strong against one adversary can be weak against
the other, and the two adversaries induce different rankings. Second, we
introduce \emph{DA-Hybrid}, a density-adaptive mechanism that combines bounded
graph $k$-anonymity with a graph-constrained differential-privacy fallback whose
budget strengthens in sparse regions; it guarantees 100\,\% availability and
on-graph outputs while achieving the lowest spatial error among all
full-availability mechanisms. We evaluate five classical mechanisms and DA-Hybrid
on a \emph{real} central-Beijing OpenStreetMap road network under two real
mobility datasets (GeoLife and T-Drive), together with a radio-technology
sensitivity analysis of the energy model. Rankings under the adversary metric are
shown---not merely asserted---to be stable from the grid abstraction to the real
network, while service availability is found to be dataset-density-driven rather
than mechanism-intrinsic. The complete framework is released for reproducible
extension.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices in smart
cities produces an unprecedented volume of fine-grained spatiotemporal data.
Smartphones, connected vehicles, and environmental sensors continuously report
location information that enables urban analytics, traffic management, and
emergency response, yet simultaneously exposes home and workplace locations,
health-facility visits, and detailed mobility, even after identifiers are
removed \cite{sweeney2002}.

Two decades of research have produced diverse mechanisms---spatial
$k$-anonymity \cite{gruteser2003}, differential privacy \cite{dwork2006},
density-aware adaptation \cite{niu2014}, graph-constrained perturbation
\cite{bordenabe2014}, and temporal cloaking \cite{gruteser2003}. But each is
typically evaluated on its own dataset, with its own metric, under its own threat
model, so a practitioner cannot obtain a defensible answer to ``which mechanism
should I deploy?'' A first response is to place all mechanisms on a shared
substrate. We argue that this is necessary but not sufficient: the privacy axis
itself must be made rigorous and threat-model-aware, and the empirical study must
run on real road networks and more than one dataset before its conclusions can be
trusted. This paper does both, and additionally contributes a new mechanism that
the rigorous evaluation shows to occupy an otherwise-empty operating point.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An adversary-grounded, threat-model-aware privacy metric}
(Section~\ref{sec:adversary}). We define privacy as the expected localisation
error of an optimal Bayesian adversary \cite{shokri2011}, instantiated as (i)~a
snapshot adversary and (ii)~a Viterbi trajectory adversary using a first-order
Markov mobility prior estimated from data. This yields a single physical unit
(metres) comparable across families, and it exposes that the two threat models
rank mechanisms \emph{differently}---evidence that the widely used single privacy
scale is invalid.
\item \textbf{DA-Hybrid, a new density-adaptive mechanism}
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}). Per report, DA-Hybrid uses the local user density to
choose bounded graph $k$-anonymity where density permits and a graph-constrained
DP fallback (with density-adaptive $\varepsilon$) otherwise. It gives a
per-report formal guarantee everywhere, 100\,\% availability by construction, and
on-graph outputs, and it attains the lowest spatial error among all
full-availability mechanisms.
\item \textbf{A real-network, multi-dataset, energy-sensitive evaluation}
(Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}). We evaluate on a real
central-Beijing OpenStreetMap road graph under both GeoLife and T-Drive, and
sweep the energy model across BLE/Zigbee/LoRa/NB-IoT/LTE-M radios, separating
robust conclusions from radio- or granularity-specific ones.
\item \textbf{An open, extensible framework} releasing all mechanisms,
adversaries, datasets, and analysis for reproducibility.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Pushkal-Gupta/Graph-based-Location-Privacy-IoT}}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================
\textbf{Spatial cloaking and $k$-anonymity.} Sweeney \cite{sweeney2002} defined
$k$-anonymity; Gruteser and Grunwald \cite{gruteser2003} introduced spatial
cloaking, and Mokbel~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{mokbel2006} and Duckham and Kulik
\cite{duckham2005} formalised region growth on spatial structures and connected
subgraphs. Recent work extends cloaking for edge computing and trajectory
publication \cite{jiang2024}, but assumes Euclidean space and fixed parameters
and is rarely compared across families.

\textbf{Differential privacy for location.} The Laplace mechanism
\cite{dwork2006calibrating} and geo-indistinguishability \cite{andres2013,
chatzikokolakis2013} add calibrated noise to coordinates; Xiao and Xiong
\cite{xiao2015} showed that temporal correlation degrades these guarantees.
Unconstrained noise can place reports off the road network, motivating
graph projection \cite{bordenabe2014,cunningham2021}.

\textbf{Trajectory privacy and evaluation.} Chow and Mokbel \cite{chow2011}
formalised trajectory privacy; recent LDP synthesis \cite{du2023,hu2024} and
generative approaches \cite{buchholz2024} advance within single families.
Crucially, the standard way to \emph{measure} location privacy is the expected
error of an optimal adversary \cite{shokri2011}---the view we adopt here in place
of family-specific scores. Prior comparative studies fix a dataset or a metric
but not the threat model, and none, to our knowledge, evaluate a common mechanism
set under both a snapshot and a trajectory adversary on a real road network
across two datasets, which is the gap this paper fills.

% =====================================================================
\section{System Model and Threat Models}
\label{sec:sysmodel}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Graph-Based Spatial Model}
The urban environment is an undirected weighted graph $G=(V,E)$ where vertices
are spatial locations and edges are road connectivity, with edge weights the
metric road distance. We use two graphs over central Beijing (latitude
$[39.8,40.1]$, longitude $[116.2,116.5]$): a controlled $30\times30$ grid (900
nodes) that removes topological confounds, and a \emph{real} OpenStreetMap drive
network for the same region, consolidated to 3{,}154 nodes and 5{,}111 edges with
irregular degree (mean 3.24, maximum 23), dead-ends (16\,\%), and hierarchy---
properties absent from the grid. The canonical distance is the shortest path;
distances are shared across all mechanisms so no mechanism is advantaged by a
different metric.

\subsection{Trajectory Data}
We use two real datasets. \emph{GeoLife} \cite{zheng2009,zheng2008} contains GPS
traces from 182 users in Beijing (2007--2012); after bounding-box filtering and a
200\,km/h speed filter it yields $\sim$16.9\,M records on the grid and $\sim$11.4\,M
map-matched to the real graph. \emph{T-Drive} is a denser one-week trace of
$\sim$10{,}000 Beijing taxis, yielding $\sim$4\,M records map-matched to the same
real graph. Using two datasets with very different density is essential: it
separates genuine mechanism properties from single-dataset quirks
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Two Threat Models}
\label{sec:threats}
Privacy is meaningful only against an adversary. We consider two, so that
mechanisms defending different threats are each judged against the threat they
target:
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{snapshot adversary} that observes a single released report and
infers the true node; and
\item a \emph{trajectory adversary} that observes a sequence of reports for a
user and reconstructs the trajectory using a mobility model.
\end{itemize}
Both possess public background knowledge estimated from the data: a population
prior $\pi(v)$ (node-occupancy distribution) and a first-order Markov mobility
model $P(v_{t+1}\mid v_t)$. The adversary knows the mechanism but not its private
randomness. Section~\ref{sec:adversary} makes both operational.

% =====================================================================
\section{Adversary-Grounded Privacy Metric}
\label{sec:adversary}
% =====================================================================
Comparative studies commonly reduce privacy to a mechanism-specific score in
$[0,1]$---e.g.\ $k/k_{\max}$ for $k$-anonymity and a log-scaled $\varepsilon$ for
DP. Such scores have no common operational meaning and cannot answer whether
$k{=}3$ anonymity hides a user better than $\varepsilon{=}1$
geo-indistinguishability. We instead define privacy as the expected error of an
optimal adversary that inverts the mechanism using public knowledge
\cite{shokri2011}, measured in metres and therefore comparable across all
mechanisms.

\subsection{Snapshot Adversary}
Given one release $o$, the adversary forms the posterior
$P(v\mid o)\propto P(o\mid v)\,\pi(v)$ over the true node $v$ and outputs the
Bayes estimate
\begin{equation}
\hat v \;=\; \arg\min_{v'\in V}\ \sum_{v\in V} P(v\mid o)\,d(v',v),
\end{equation}
minimising expected graph distance. Privacy is
$\mathrm{AE}_{\mathrm{snap}}=\mathbb{E}[d(v_{\text{true}},\hat v)]$ in metres.
The observation model $P(o\mid v)$ differs by family---for cloaking mechanisms it
restricts the prior to the released anonymity set; for the planar-Laplace
mechanisms it is the Laplace emission density times the prior---but the measured
quantity is identical for all.

\subsection{Trajectory Adversary}
A stronger adversary observes a \emph{sequence} of releases for a user and decodes
the most likely hidden node sequence by Viterbi, combining the per-report
emission model with the Markov mobility prior. Privacy is the mean tracking error
$\mathrm{AE}_{\mathrm{traj}}$ (metres) and the per-step re-identification rate.
This is the threat temporal mechanisms target, and the one that independent
per-report noise fails to resist because temporal correlation lets the adversary
average noise out \cite{xiao2015}.

\subsection{Why a Single Scale Is Inadequate}
The two adversaries induce \emph{different} rankings
(Section~\ref{sec:results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:dual}): a mechanism strong against one
can be weak against the other, so any single-scale privacy comparison mis-ranks
mechanisms defending different threats. This is the central methodological point
of the paper.

% =====================================================================
\section{Privacy Mechanisms}
\label{sec:mechanisms}
% =====================================================================
We evaluate five representative classical mechanisms spanning three families,
plus our DA-Hybrid (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).

\textbf{Graph $k$-anonymity} grows a connected region by breadth-first search
until it covers $\ge k$ users, and reports the region medoid
\cite{mokbel2006,duckham2005} (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}). \textbf{Coordinate
differential privacy} adds planar Laplace noise $\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon)$
to each coordinate \cite{andres2013}; the output may fall off the road network.
\textbf{Graph-constrained DP} projects the noisy point to the nearest graph node
\cite{bordenabe2014}, restoring on-graph validity. \textbf{Density-aware adaptive
$k$-anonymity} selects $k$ per node from local density percentiles
\cite{niu2014}. \textbf{Temporal cloaking} delays reports until $k$ users share a
temporal window and releases the group medoid \cite{gruteser2003}.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{fig1_spatial_cloaking.png}
\caption{Spatial cloaking on the Beijing graph: true locations (left) are
replaced by the medoid of a connected region containing at least $k$ users
(right).}
\label{fig:spatial}
\end{figure}

\section{DA-Hybrid: Density-Adaptive Hybrid Privacy}
\label{sec:hybrid}
% =====================================================================
The comparison reveals a gap: $k$-anonymity attains low error where users are
dense but loses availability in sparse areas, while DP guarantees availability at
an error premium. DA-Hybrid closes this gap with a per-report, density-driven
controller (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hybrid}).

For a user at node $v$, let $\rho(v)$ be the local density (users at $v$ plus its
one-hop neighbours). The mechanism (i)~attempts bounded graph $k$-anonymity,
growing a region until it covers $\ge k$ users or reaches a density-dependent
size cap; if $k$ is met it reports the medoid (low error, on-graph,
$k$-satisfied). Otherwise (ii)~it falls back to graph-constrained DP with a
density-adaptive budget---$\varepsilon$ is reduced in sparse regions, precisely
where anonymity is hardest and most needed---and projects to the nearest node.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DA-Hybrid per-report anonymisation}
\label{alg:hybrid}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE snapshot $S$, target $k$, base budget $\varepsilon$, size cap $R$
\FOR{each user $u$ at node $v$}
  \STATE $\rho \leftarrow$ local density of $v$ in $S$
  \STATE grow region $\mathcal{R}$ by BFS until $|users(\mathcal{R})|\ge k$ or $|\mathcal{R}|\ge R(\rho)$
  \IF{$|users(\mathcal{R})|\ge k$}
     \STATE report $\mathrm{medoid}(\mathcal{R})$ \COMMENT{$k$-anonymity branch}
  \ELSE
     \STATE $\varepsilon' \leftarrow$ density-adaptive budget ($\downarrow$ in sparse regions)
     \STATE add $\mathrm{Lap}(\Delta/\varepsilon')$ noise; project to nearest node \COMMENT{DP fallback}
  \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Because the fallback never fails, \emph{availability is 100\,\% by
construction}; because dense reports are served by cloaking, error stays low
where density permits; and every output is a valid road-network node carrying a
per-report guarantee of one of two well-understood types. The cost matches the
underlying primitives (bounded BFS, or $O(1)$ noise plus nearest-node
projection).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% =====================================================================
All mechanisms run on the same graph and data with a shared distance layer. We
sweep $k\in\{2,\dots,6\}$, $\varepsilon\in\{0.1,0.5,1,2,5\}$, and time windows
$\{60,300,600,1200\}$\,s; unless noted, results use the representative 10-minute
window. Privacy is measured by the two adversaries of
Section~\ref{sec:adversary}; availability is the fraction of reports served with
their guarantee; utility is the spatial (graph) location error. The adversary
prior and Markov mobility model are estimated once per dataset and shared. On the
real graph a single dense distance matrix is shared by every mechanism and the
adversary, keeping the exact Bayes estimator tractable.

\textbf{Energy model.} Per-report energy is
$E=E_{\text{radio}}+E_{\text{comp}}+E_{\text{retrans}}$, with
$E_{\text{radio}}=P_{\text{tx}}t_{\text{tx}}$. Rather than fix a single radio, we
sweep BLE, Zigbee, LoRa, NB-IoT, and LTE-M
(Section~\ref{sec:energy}) so that radio-dependent conclusions are identified as
such.

\subsection{Real Road Network and Second Dataset}
\label{sec:realnet}
We repeat the evaluation on the real OSM graph with GeoLife map-matched to it, and
again on the denser T-Drive taxis (Table~\ref{tab:cross},
Fig.~\ref{fig:cross}). Two robust conclusions emerge. \emph{Rankings hold across
topology:} under the adversary-privacy metric the ordering on the real graph is
nearly identical to the grid---temporal cloaking most private, DA-Hybrid most
utility-favouring---so relative rankings are \emph{shown}, not merely asserted, to
survive the grid abstraction; only the DP/graph-constrained-DP pair exchanges
places. \emph{Availability is dataset-density-driven, not mechanism-intrinsic:}
on sparse GeoLife, $k$-anonymity (74.5\,\%) and temporal cloaking (18.5\,\%)
suffer denial of service, but on dense T-Drive \emph{every} mechanism reaches
100\,\% availability. Absolute spatial error scales with graph granularity---the
Laplace scale is calibrated to the median edge length, which is shorter on the
real graph---so error is compared within, not across, topologies.

\subsection{Energy-Model Sensitivity}
\label{sec:energy}
The claim that radio transmission dominates per-report energy is not universal:
it depends on the radio (Fig.~\ref{fig:energy}). Sweeping BLE (0.05\,mJ) through
NB-IoT/LTE-M (60--75\,mJ), the radio share ranges from $6$--$50\%$ under
BLE---where the BFS-based mechanisms' computation dominates---to $99\%$ under
cellular IoT. The ``radio dominates'' conclusion is therefore scoped to
LPWAN/cellular deployments; for short-range radios, algorithm computation is a
first-order energy factor.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================
\textbf{Deployment guidance.} The right mechanism depends on the binding
constraint. Where denial of service is unacceptable, use DA-Hybrid or a DP variant
(100\,\% availability); DA-Hybrid additionally keeps outputs on-graph and gives
the lowest error among these. Where the strongest privacy against a
single-observation attacker is paramount and some service loss is tolerable,
$k$-anonymity or temporal cloaking rank highest---but only against the
\emph{snapshot} adversary; under tracking their advantage shrinks, so the choice
must be made against the relevant threat. Availability requirements should be read
against deployment density: on dense fleets (T-Drive-like) all mechanisms serve
every user, so availability ceases to discriminate.

\textbf{Limitations.} Absolute error is not comparable across topologies because
the Laplace scale is graph-granularity dependent; our cross-topology conclusions
therefore rest on adversary-privacy and availability. The energy model is
analytical, suited to ranking rather than absolute prediction, and we report its
radio sensitivity explicitly. The mobility prior is first-order Markov; richer
priors would strengthen the trajectory adversary and can only lower the reported
privacy.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================
We replaced ad-hoc privacy scores with an adversary-grounded metric evaluated
under two threat models, showing that a single privacy scale mis-ranks mechanisms
that defend different threats. Using this metric we introduced DA-Hybrid, a
density-adaptive mechanism with 100\,\% availability, on-graph outputs, and the
lowest error among full-availability mechanisms. Across a real Beijing road
network and two datasets, adversary-privacy rankings are stable while service
availability is shown to be dataset-density-driven rather than mechanism-
intrinsic. The framework is released for reproducible extension.
